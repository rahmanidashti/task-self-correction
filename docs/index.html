Self-Correcting Large Language Models: Generation vs. Multiple Choice

Hossein A. Rahmani1, Satyapriya Krishna2*, Xi Wang3, Mohammadmehdi Naghiaei4, Emine Yilmaz1

1University College London  ·  2Amazon AGI  ·  3University of Sheffield  ·  4University of Southern California

*Work was done prior to joining Amazon.

Paper Code Datasets BibTeX

TL;DR — Asking an LLM to review and revise its own answer does not work the same way for every task format. Free-form generation fixes errors quickly but starts to drift with more rounds, while multiple-choice selection stays stable but rarely recovers from a wrong first pick.

Abstract

Large language models have recently demonstrated remarkable abilities to self-correct their responses through iterative refinement, often referred to as self-consistency or self-reflection. However, the dynamics of this self-correction mechanism may differ substantially depending on whether the model is tasked with open-ended text generation or with selecting the most appropriate response from multiple predefined options. In this paper, we conduct a systematic investigation of these two paradigms by comparing performance trends and error-correction behaviors across various natural language understanding and reasoning tasks, covering language models of different scales and families. Our experimental results reveal distinct patterns of improvement and failure modes:

While open-ended generation often benefits from the flexibility of re-interpretation and compositional refinement, multiple-choice selection can leverage clearer solution boundaries but may be limited by the provided options.

This contrast also reflects the dual demands faced by emerging agentic LLM applications: effective agents must not only generate and refine open-ended plans or explanations, but also make reliable discrete choices when operating within constrained action spaces. Our findings, therefore, highlight that the design of self-correction mechanisms should take into account the interaction between task structure and output space, with implications for both knowledge-intensive reasoning and decision-oriented applications of LLMs.

How we study it

Same questions, two output spaces

We use benchmarks that ask each question in two parallel forms, so the only thing that changes is the shape of the answer the model has to produce.

Open-ended generation

The model writes its answer freely. A revision can restructure arguments, add missing details, or reinterpret the question — and can also wander away from it.

Multiple-choice selection

The model picks one of the given options. A revision can only move between those options, which keeps answers stable but makes an early mistake hard to undo.

Datasets
DisambiguationQA from BIG-Bench Extra Hard (resolving ambiguous pronouns, reasoning-oriented) and tinyTruthfulQA (short factual questions that invite misconceptions, knowledge-oriented)
Models
SmolLM2-1.7B, Qwen2.5-3B, Llama-3.1-8B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, Qwen2.5-14B, Gemini-2.0-Flash
Prompting
Baseline, zero-shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT), and self-consistency (SC)
Evaluation
TruthfulQA truth judge (tinyTruthfulQA) and GPT-4o as judge (DisambiguationQA) for generation; matching the gold option for multiple choice
Findings

What changes between the two formats

1

Generation adapts, then drifts

Most gains come in the first one or two rounds, as models fix obvious errors or fill in missing facts. Later revisions increasingly replace correct answers with incorrect ones.

2

Multiple choice is stable, but inert

Answers flip rarely, so correct answers stay correct. But when the first choice is wrong, later rounds seldom move to the right option — a form of logit inertia.

3

Task difficulty sets the stakes

Larger models and reasoning prompts help most on the harder DisambiguationQA. On tinyTruthfulQA, the three prompting strategies give nearly overlapping curves.

4

More rounds are not more progress

Across models, prompts, and formats, accuracy mostly plateaus after the first one or two iterations instead of improving steadily.

Implications

Designing self-correction for the task

Neither paradigm is universally better: generation trades stability for adaptability, and multiple choice trades recoverability for stability.

  • Stable outputs. Applications that need stable answers, such as safety-critical domains, benefit from constrained correction, though extra mechanisms such as external verification or re-ranking may be needed to overcome inertia.
  • Catching every correction. Tasks where recovering from errors matters most may favor open-ended revision, provided there are safeguards against drift.
  • Hybrid strategies. Use generation to explore candidate answers, then constrained verification to anchor correctness.
  • Knowing when to stop. Dynamic stopping rules can halt iteration once improvements saturate or harmful drift is detected.
